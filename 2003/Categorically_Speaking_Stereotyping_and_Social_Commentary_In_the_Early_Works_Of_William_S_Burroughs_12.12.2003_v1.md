# Categorically Speaking:
## Stereotyping and Social Commentary In the Early Works Of William S Burroughs

**Nate Osit**  
Prof. Holladay  
Literature of the Beat Movement  
December 12, 2003  
Version 1  
**Status:** Historical literature essay

> **Historical note:** This essay was written in 2003, when the author identified as a queer man. It discusses homosexuality, queer subcultures, stigma, and William S. Burroughs’ representations of gay men using terminology drawn both from the source texts and from the language of the period. Some language and interpretations would be framed differently today. The essay is preserved without substantive revision as a record of the author’s thinking at the time.

In the books *Queer* and *Junky*, by William S Burroughs, Burroughs uses stereotyping to show the extremes of American and World culture at the time. His characters can generally be put into one general category, and then be organized into sub-categories. The categories assigned to characters almost always carry with them a stigma that the character is forced to bear. He does this very methodically, because he wrote these books in the mindset of an anthropologist reporting on an entire culture, but also wanted to show the vile nature of his world. Using this method, it becomes necessary to analyze the stereotypes he uses, and those he invents, to understand the context of the novels, because it goes beyond the standard use of characters in fiction novels.

Being from the middle class himself, Burroughs is able to give social commentary on the typical American middle-class and bourgeois mindset. Burroughs defines the upper middle class as “a composite of negatives…largely delineated by what he is not.”(Junky 38) In Junky, the middle class are not mentioned often, but when he does mention them he sees them as mere targets for crime. His lack of reference to the class can be taken as implying that they were merely part of the masses, and had no effect on society, or at least his junk driven one. He plays down his own middle-class background, because he wanted to become engulfed in his new, exciting drug and crime culture. He receives an allowance of $150 a month, which was more than enough money to live on at the time, but he seeks out drugs and crime regardless of this cushion. This shows his desire to step out from the masses and in doing so gain character and experience. In fact, he must leave his background behind in order to pursue the life he chose for himself.

In Queer, Burroughs’ view of the American upper middle class changes to a more worldly view. In the book his character, William Lee, takes on the role of the rich American in Mexico, and assumes everything is for sale, at a price. The object he wishes to attain is named Eugene Allerton, a man who is not a homosexual by nature. But Lee proceeds to infect Allerton’s life; he does him favors for which he expects favors in return, teaches him using literary knowledge, and slowly enters Allerton’s subconscious by cleverly using pronouns to indicate them to be a couple. An example of this is when Lee describes his idea for a restaurant that sells live flaming pigs, when he uses the phrase “Or, if we run a dramatic joint”(Queer 52) instead of the singular “If I run a dramatic joint.” These gestures get under Allerton’s skin, and before he knows it he is engaging in sexual relationships with Lee. Lee then continues the relationship by helping Allerton buy back his camera from a pawn shop, a favor which indebts Allerton greatly. He ends up going to South America with Lee, and having to engage in sex twice a week. Lee’s extravagant yet subversive dealings show the American capitalist manner used to get what one wants no matter the apparent availability, or lack thereof.

In both books, references to women in Lee’s life are kept to a bare minimum, and the few that are included show a very sharp contrast in his perception of them. The two most important women in the books are his Wife, who was not named, and the drug lord of Mexico City named Lupita. His wife is a loving, giving individual who shares his love of drugs and literature. She is always there when he needs help, and is the one who bails him out in New York, and is waiting at the door with some goofballs to help his junk sickness. She embodies everything he finds tolerable and good in women, which apparently isn’t too much. On the other hand, Lupita is portrayed as a vile creature. She weighs 300 pounds, has a thin face, and runs a monopoly of the junk business in Mexico City. Showing Lupita as being disgusting and greedy reflects on Burroughs’ general disgust with women, and is the reason he turned to homosexuality. Although Lupita is shown as repulsive and Lee’s wife is scarcely mentioned, women generally got off easy in Junky, at least in comparison to other groups.

Although Burroughs himself was a homosexual, he did not hesitate to paint a very condescending portrait of the “fag” culture. He uses the stereotype of an effeminate, limp wristed queer, and describes them as being less than human. They were “like puppets on invisible strings, galvanized into hideous activity that is the negation of everything living and spontaneous…the live human being has moved out of these bodies long ago.”(Junky 60) Despite giving such a harsh description of these creatures, Lee goes to the bar and ends up going to a hotel with one of the queers at the bar. This seemingly hypocritical nature shows that that even the culture is generally filled by disgusting people, he cannot deny his own urges. And in Queer, his mockery of the fag culture and his more masculine behavior are attempts to make Allerton, the object of his desire, feel as though engaging in sexual acts with Lee would not make him a homosexual. Here, Lee succeeds in his efforts, and Allerton has sex with Lee without identifying himself as a homosexual. Burroughs used these tactics to separate homosexuals into two groups. There were those who were fairies, ones who would usually be seen as the norm for queers. Then there were the William Lee’s, the ones who did not share any attributes with the fags except for their desire for other men. This distinction would be very important for Burroughs to make, because he would not want to be associated with the degenerates of the former.

In Junky, the government’s fears about narcotics seem to be baseless and paranoiac. The time period in which the book takes place is coincidental to the beginnings of McCarthyism in the US, and the same fear of Communism transferred over to drug addicts. The first example of this is in New Orleans, where Lee meets a man in Frank’s Bar. He begins talking to the man, and the topic of Narcotics is brought up. The man is quite convinced that the people responsible for spreading Communism in the US are the same who were responsible for distributing narcotics. During this time, baseless accusations were all the rage, and this man was obviously eager to get in on the action. Soon after this incident, it became against the law to be a drug addict, and cops were able to stop people on the street and look for tracks on their arms. If any were found, the addict would be pressured to sign a statement admitting guilt, enabling the cops to charge them under the drug addict law. To anyone who believes in freedom, this is a serious infringement on personal rights, and Burroughs portrays the offenders as obsessed witch-hunters, looking for proof where there is none to be found. He even goes so far as to compare this new anti-narcotics drive to Nazi anti-Semitism. Burroughs does not see why some addictions, like alcohol or power, are tolerated or considered a disease, while addicts of narcotics are considered criminals and degenerates. While Lee is in Mexico, he is relieved to be out of the crazy and obsessed US.

The desperation of junkies in these books is matched only by their ability to focus on their one goal; the goal of getting more junk to feed their habit. Everything in between fixes is just time to be killed. When Lee started using heroin, he stopped going out to drink, didn’t eat as much, didn’t feel like having as many conversations as before, and even stopped bathing. His singular goal in life at that time was to get money and then to get junk. The addicts life “telescopes down to junk, one fix looking forward to the next…he does not realize that he is just going through the motions in his non-junk activities.” (Junky 19) This leaves little room for sympathy in the world of Junk, but it can be found amongst dealers who cannot turn down someone who is junk sick. Since they have probably been sick themselves, they are able to display a small amount of empathy for the fellow addict. But on the other hand, if one is so desperate for junk that he simply cannot stand it, there was the option of going to the police and ratting on fellow addicts. This seemed to Lee a crime worse than murder, and gave no respect to anyone who would be a pigeon. One man, Roy, was forced to rat out an old time addict who was a friend of his. He had always said “I don’t see how a pigeon can live with himself”(Junky 125), and lived up to his word when he killed himself. The act was portrayed by Burroughs as the right thing to do in the situation, and in doing so established his condescending view toward informants. Although the main focuses of the books were junkies and junk culture, many of the ideas are applicable to many larger groups, and maybe even the world as a whole.

The world depicted in Burroughs’ writing is one of obsession, desperation, and need. Everyone in his world wants something from everybody, and its parasitic nature is portrayed in its most vile and sickening form, that of the junky. But the world of Junk is too small to contain this virus, and it spills out into every corner of society. Only by making the characters in his books conform to certain stereotypes could he truly convey the symbolic nature of this virus. While the junky has to have a certain amount of junk to get his fix, others in the book have unending needs and obsessions; the police cannot arrest every drug addict in the country, consumers cannot buy their happiness with money, the government cannot contain a vast army of imaginary communists, and Americans cannot protect their children from all the horrors of the world. But these addictions and desperate attempts to grasp unattainable goals display a condition much worse than heroin addiction, which is merely a disease involving substance abuse. Using his categories and stereotypes, Burroughs has portrayed a symbolic yet raw view of the world which is still quite relevant to this day.
