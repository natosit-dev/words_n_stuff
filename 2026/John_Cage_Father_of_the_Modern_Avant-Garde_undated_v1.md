# John Cage:
## Father of the Modern Avant-Garde

**Nate Osit**  
Modern World  
Original date: Undated, college-era  
Archive publication: September 5, 2026  
Version 1  
**Status:** Historical music essay

*This is an archival publication of the original college paper. The original document does not provide a date. Historical claims, wording, interpretations, and period-specific references are preserved without substantive revision.*

John Cage was born on September 5, 1912, in Los Angeles, and died in New York in 1992.

During his life he revolutionized classical music and introduced the world to new and daring forms of music. He broke all available boundaries in search of new sounds and arrangements that would stretch people’s perceptions of what music is, and in doing so became one of the most prominent figures in the avant-garde Minimalist movement of art and music. The Minimalist art movement occurred in the mid-20th century, and the work produced was reduced to only what was absolutely necessary, and made frequent use of repetition in music. But his music also went to new heights in the realm of avant-garde music, mainly because his use of chance as a way to compose music was very experimental. Cage believed that over the hundreds of years that classical music had been composed there had been quite enough beautiful music made, and it was time for people to recognize the sounds that might not always be quite as appealing.

John Cage started out as a traditional classical music composer, and accomplished a few notable works, but it was after he abandoned traditional harmony and delved into the experimental that his work became really new and innovative. When told he wouldn’t be able to create music this way, that he would come up against a wall, he replied “I will beat my head against that wall”. He did just that, and completely changed the way people thought about the relationship between composer, performer, and audience.

His most famous piece, 4’33”, was simply four minutes and thirty three seconds of a musician sitting at a piano doing “nothing”. But by doing nothing, Cage challenged audiences to accept that there is no silence, and that the performer was doing just as much as any other musician. The music came from the environment in the concert hall, and the unintentional noises made by audience members and the hall itself. Cage came up with these ideas about silence when he visited an anechoic chamber at Harvard University, a device used to completely eliminate all sound. In the chamber, Cage heard two sounds, one his blood circulating and the other his nervous system. He then realized that silence is impossible, and that was the basis for 4’33”.

Much of his work is based upon chance, uncontrollable variables inserted into his work to remove ego, and engineered to make people simply accept things as they come out and not try and change them. His first piece for prepared piano was entitled “Bacchanale”, and was written in 1938. At this point in time he had come to accept his personal schism with harmony, and began to embrace percussion and chance. He was working as an accompanist to a dance group at a school in Seattle, Washington, and wanted to write a percussion piece. However, there was no percussion ensemble, and he had to write it for piano. So he began placing objects such as steel plates and nails into pianos to achieve percussive sounds, and the Prepared Piano was born. The objects did not give a standard sound, so each performance differed greatly in the sounds used. His ideas about music were based on more Eastern ideas like Zen Buddhism, and increased their popularity in Western countries.

Cage is responsible for a lot of the music that comes out today, because he was the first person to use turntables and other electronic devices when composing music. Many of the popular ambient minimalists of today such as Phillip Glass and Steve Reich cite him as a huge influence on the way they make music, and his music is still performed by various orchestras. Cage’s “Four Seasons: A ballet in one act” was recently performed at the Boston Symphony Orchestra over the summer, and was very heavily influenced by Asian themes. And another piece, entitled “As Slow As Possible” is currently being played in Germany. The piece was composed in 1992 for piano, but it is being performed on the town organ in Halberstadt. It was originally supposed to be about 20 minutes long, but the version in Germany is set to last 639 years. The first chord was struck on September 5th, in 2001, and another is struck about once every year. At the current rate, the song will be completed in 2640.

John Cage was the son of an inventor, and he carried on the tradition by inventing new ways of composing music, and even newer ways of listening and perceiving music. He has opened the realm of music to include every sound, and made nature the world’s greatest composer.
