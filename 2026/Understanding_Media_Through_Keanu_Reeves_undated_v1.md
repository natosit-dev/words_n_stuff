# Media, Technology, and Society: McLuhan and his relevance today
## (Or “Understanding Media Through Keanu Reeves”)

**Nat Osit**  
Original date: Undated, college-era  
Archive publication: September 1, 2026  
Version 1  
**Status:** Historical writing sample

> “But, soft! What light through yonder window breaks?”  
> —Romeo and Juliet

*This is an archival publication of the original college paper. The prose, claims, examples, and period-specific references are preserved without substantive revision.*

Technology is a process that evolves seemingly without our input, yet it affects every single aspect of our lives. The media projects images that are taken as reality by the user, and shapes the user's perception of reality to fit the images that are being projected. There are a lot of issues surrounding media and technology, but much of the debate that goes on today would not have been possible were it not for a media theorist from the 60's, Marshall McLuhan. I plan to investigate McLuhan's ideas in more depth, and see how they relate to modern technology, media, and other media theorists. The first three concepts I will explore are “the medium is the message,” “the tetrad,” and “the global village.” These are the three key ideas in McLuhan's work, so I think it is a good place to start.

The first concept I will explore is McLuhan's idea that “the medium is the message.” This phrase comes from his book “Understanding Media”, and is an idea that makes the study of media much more important than merely analyzing the content that is projected by various media. The basic idea is that a medium is more important than the content it conveys, and that the impact media has on society is based on the type of medium. For an example, McLuhan uses the medium of the light bulb. Although it has no content, its introduction into society created space where once there was none. It does this by lighting up places that would otherwise be dark. Another more modern example is the television. Even though there is importance in what content is transmitted through television, the fact that it is a medium with little or no interaction, and tends to induce apathy in its user, has far more impact than whether the content broadcast is sports, violence, or educational programming. Even though this idea is still very important, I think that we may have gone beyond this concept in our technological evolution, and I will discuss this idea in relation to French theorist Jean Baudrillard later in the paper.

The second concept vital to an understanding of McLuhan's work is his idea of the tetrad, which contains 4 laws of media. “Visually, a tetrad consists of five diamonds forming an X, with the name of media listed in its centre. (wikipedia)” The four laws are enhancement, retrieval, reversal, and obsolescence. The laws are divided into two groups, figure and ground. Retrieval and enhancement fall under figure, while reversal and obsolescence are grouped together as ground. These groupings help show the relationship the concepts have to each other. Obsolescence is the decay of contemporary media to the point where they become obsolete, or at least appear to be so, and is the transition from figure to ground. Reversal is what happens when a new media is hyped too much, and the effects that once were positive become negative. Retrieval is when a media reaches back into the past to re-use ideas. This is part of McLuhan's “rear-view mirror” concept of media, and shows how much new media is simply a reflection of old media. Retrieval is also the transition from ground to figure, as it explains how the media evolves. The final law of the tetrad theory is enhancement, which is the amplification of the new media as it is used to solve new problems and engage its users in new and innovative ways. Through these laws, McLuhan has a way of systematically analyzing media, and these laws can be applied to the new media that are developing today.

The third concept that is important to understanding McLuhan is that of the “global village”, which, in 1967, was a revolutionary idea preceding the invention of the Internet. McLuhan postulates that “'Time' has ceased, 'space' has vanished. We now live in a global village...a simultaneous happening. ... We have begun again to structure the primordial feeling, the tribal emotions from which a few centuries of literacy divorced us.”(“The Medium is the Massage”) The idea of technology shaping the world into one that is instantaneous and connected is even more relevant with today's technology and media. However, more important is the idea that this new technology brings us back to relate with others in a way that was not possible before electronic media; that literacy and the printing press had served to isolate man, even while it created the “public.” This idea is certainly debatable, and I plan to debate it later in the paper.

After briefly going over McLuhan's major concepts, we are left with a lot of interesting questions and problems related to media and technology. There are also a lot of points where it would behoove us to critique McLuhan, especially his techno-idealistic beliefs and his near complete exclusion of political ideas in his media theories. There are also many more media related issues to explore, and to see how the modern individual relates to media, and how media has shaped the individual's perception of reality and community. It would also make sense to look at McLuhan's ideas and see how they are applied to modern media, and whether those concepts stand up. Needless to say, there are a lot of areas to get to, but I would like to start by analyzing McLuhan through the writings of Jean Baudrillard in his essay, “The Precession of the Simulacra,” and how media serve to shape our perception of reality and, in Baudrillard's opinion, shape reality itself.

In his essay, Baudrillard talks about simulacra, which are defined as “an unreal or vague representation”. He uses the idea of the simulacrum to show how our compulsion to classify things to fit into our perception of reality leads to the signs that are being used to lose their true meaning, and eventually the signs dictate reality. This new reality shaped by simulacra is what he refers to as “hyperreality.” I think it's important to define hyperreal, just to clarify what Baudrillard is talking about. He defines it as “the generation of models of a real without origin or reality.” This means that all of our desires and perceptions of reality are shaped by simulacra, or a “precession of simulacra”, “the product of an irradiating synthesis of combinatory models in a hyperspace without atmosphere.” Images no longer indicated or reflect a basic reality; rather, images become reality and a simulation of a simulation. This can be seen as a critique of McLuhan's concept of “the medium is the message,” since in Baudrillard's theory it is the message which becomes the medium. Rather than images being projected onto a television screen, it is the television which projects images onto the user. The television shapes the user's perception of reality, and as there is nothing outside our perceptions, it shapes reality itself. As media develops, and Adorno's “culture industry” infiltrates every aspect of our lives, reality becomes less real, and is classified away into the hyperreal. In relation to his ideas on the “successive phases of the image,” we have entered the final stage, where the image “bears no relation to any reality whatever: it is its own pure simulacrum.” In a time where we cannot escape media, advertising, and the culture industry, Baudrillard insists that “simulation threatens the difference between 'true' and 'false', between 'real' and 'imaginary'.” Media and technology constantly blast us with images that bear no relation to reality, yet we accept it as such. There is nothing left outside our own perception of reality, and our perception of reality is based on the hyperreal imagery that is projected onto us. Media, in Baudrillard's opinion, have become reality, and it is our obsession with these extensions of ourselves that traps us in a world of hyperreality. It leaves us alone, isolated, in “the desert of the real”, which is also a quote from the popular Keanu Reeves film, “The Matrix”, a film dealing with perceptions of reality. Baudrillard has said they misunderstood his writing, however, and didn't choose to work with him on any of the director's projects.

Before moving on to the next critique of McLuhan's work, I would like to explore his tetrad theory in more depth, and see how it relates to modern technology. As stated before, the theory contains four laws, those of enhancement, retrieval, reversal, and obsolescence. It is important to see this as it relates to specific media, and not just as a theory. So I will analyze computers using the tetrad theory, and see if there are any complications that arise with the analysis.

First, what does the computer render obsolete by its introduction? Well, a number of things have become obsolete as the the result of the information age, but most notably the physical labor needed to gain information and the printing press have become much less important. Because of the speed of computers, it is no longer necessary to go to the library to research information, as much information is already available on the Internet. Even though books are still printed, it is a process that is no longer really necessary. Next, reversal; what are some effects of this new medium that once may have been positive but now are negative results? One negative result is the elimination of the need for retention and work, as any information that is needed can be accessed instantly without any real work going into it. This encourages the user to rely heavily on the medium for information without going through any processes to retain the information because it is all readily available. Like writing in the myth of Theuth from the Platonic dialogue with Phaedrus, computers serve only to remind, and are removed from reality. They do not enable remembering. Computers encourage forgetfulness in its users, making people appear more intelligent than they really are because of the information they have access to, and not based on what they actually know and comprehend. Like writing, the goal of computers, the dispersal of information, leads to forgetfulness and loss of comprehension. Another way of looking at it is that human beings become hard drives to store data, like Keanu Reeves in the popular film, “Johnny Mnemonic.”

The next part of the tetrad is retrieval, which is how the new media reflects ideas and media of the past. This is where the complication begins, because nearly all media that existed before computers are reflected in its design. The written word, photographs, movies, audio, discourse, light, and numbers are all reflected in the computer. The underlying concept, however, is the retrieval of the tribal idea of community that is inherent in the Internet. McLuhan's “global village” is perfectly reflected in the instantaneous networking made possible by digital communication through computers. Computers and the Internet eliminate the hierarchical structure of information, as all information is made available at once in any order. It allows for networking to occur between all people, no matter what physical distances may separate them. Finally, computers must be looked at in terms of how they contribute to the enhancement of media, and create new and innovative ways for problem solving. The computer may not contain truly new media, but having all media available in one interactive form enhances the relevance of all media. Computers allow its users to construct a media tool that aids them in their own lives, in whatever medium they choose. It allows users access to history, like print, but in a simpler, innovative way, not unlike the time machine in the popular Keanu Reeves film “Bill and Ted's Excellent Adventure.” Users can choose to use it as a tool for accessing print media, for communicating with others like the telephone, organize their tasks like writing, or any other medium they need. By serving as all of these media, we are brought back to the idea that this new medium makes old media obsolete. One wonders, however, if humans have become the medium as Baudrillard suggests, how long until humans become obsolete? Or are they already? Which brings me to my next theorist...

McLuhan is noted as one of the most outspoken proponents of media and technology, but his views are considered by some to be far too idealistic. He does not always take into account all of the consequences of progress, and some would criticize him quite harshly for failing to do so. One such person is noted mathematician and political theorist Theodore Kaczynski, who in his essay, “Industrial Society and Its Future” discussed the consequences of technological advances which develop unchecked and unquestioned. In the essay, Kaczynski discusses how technology is used to control human behavior and thought, and unless there is a large crash of the industrial system, this control will become absolute. This process is not one of direct totalitarian oppression, but one of gradual encroachment on human freedom. Humans are encouraged to stay within the boundaries created by society, and are punished when they attempt to break out of those roles, not unlike Keanu Reeves in the popular film, “Chain Reaction.” One example he gives is that in order to operate in society today, one must have computer skills in order to become employable. To gain such skills, Kaczynski gives the example of parents sending their kids to Sylvan Learning Center to be manipulated into becoming enthusiastic about their studies. “It may be that some of these parents wish that one didn't have to have specialized training to get a job and that their kid didn't have to be brainwashed into becoming a computer nerd. But what can they do? They can't change society, and their child may be unemployable if he doesn't have certain skills. So they send him to Sylvan.” These may seem unimportant at first, but the gradual decay of freedom is a result of needing to become plugged into the world's mixing board. He portrays the mass media as a form of release that is necessary in a world where there is constant pressure on the individual to perform according to society's roles. While some people may escape one or another particular form of mass media, it is nearly impossible to escape it all. (That is, without doing as Kaczynski did and becoming a hermit.) He refers to the time before mass media as a time when “most people got along very nicely with no other entertainment than that which each local community created for itself.” He makes the connection between all of the pressures that are put on the modern individual and the mass media, saying that it would not be possible to take away so many freedoms from people without giving them a release as abundant as mass entertainment.

As media and technology progress, they are hindered only by technical problems. There is little to no debate about whether or not a technology should be developed. This is a disturbing idea, one that Kaczynski takes to the extreme in the form of mind control. The mind is controlled by hormones and electric impulses, and the process of controlling thoughts is a mainly technical problem. It is a problem open to scientific attack, and is essentially a technical problem. He argues that as long as the process isn't introduced all at once, there will be little resistance to the idea. This leads us down a dangerous path of self destruction that we have few ways of altering. Not unlike Keanu Reeves in the popular film “Speed”, we are forced to remain on a bus that is destined for destruction, and we are incapable of slowing down. The solution that Kaczynski gives to this problem is that we need to dismantle industrial society, but this is a drastic and destructive task. He has given technology a chance to prove itself, but there is still hunger, war, corruption, and destruction. He says that no technology can survive if the revolution is to be complete, because “Never forget that the human race with technology is just like an alcoholic with a barrel of wine.” We can't resist it, and it may lead to our destruction or enslavement, not unlike the flask of concentrated evil in the popular Keanu Reeves made for TV movie, “Babes in Toyland.” We can see how robots and automation are beginning to create this dystopian technological future.

Another aspect of media and technology that is ignored by McLuhan is automation and the natural byproduct of that process, robots. One writer who explores issues regarding the effects of robots on the economy and society is another Marshall, Marshall Brain. His essay, “Robotic Nation,” and his story, “Manna,” explain in detail how robots could take over the majority of jobs in the US over the next 30-40 years, and what sort of impact that will have. We are beginning to see the introduction of robots now, with devices like ATMs and automated checkouts at retail stores and gas stations. These devices are just the tip of the iceberg, and it's only a matter of time before robots are introduced on a wide scale. We can also see the development of humanoid robots, like Honda's ASIMO. Brain predicts that by the year 2030, we will be able to buy humanoid robots that could “clean bathrooms, take out trash, wipe down tables, mop floors, sweep parking lots, mow grass and so on.” Robots do no get tired, they can work for 24 hours straight, and they will be capable of doing jobs far better than any human. This idea was portrayed in the film “I,Robot,” starring Will Smith, who turned down the role of Morpheus in the popular film “The Matrix” starring actor Keanu Reeves. According to Moore's law, which states that CPU speed doubles about every 18-24 months, computers could equal the human brain as quickly as 2040, able to process 10 quadrillion operations per second. This may seem crazy, but Marshall brings up that to say in 1900 that people would be flying from one coast to another at supersonic speeds within 50 years would also have seemed insane. The issue remains that technology proceeds without any real discussion, and that we only see technical problems to overcome. Brain postulates that nearly every construction, manufacturing, transportation, hotel and restaurant job will go to robots by the year 2040 or 2050. This would lead to unemployment rates that have never before been seen in this country, even during the great depression. All this is happening without any real public input, and people are more concerned with engaging in the mass media than they are in engaging in debate about the future of their jobs.

So how do robots relate to McLuhan's ideas about media? As demonstrated before, computers have made the age of the global village possible, even though they may eventually make humans obsolete. We should be using media to start the debate about whether we really want technological progress and if so, how best to use it. If we don't, the decisions will be left up to whoever has access to the technology, and we will continue to use technology poorly. While we evolve to a point where human labor becomes unnecessary, we could use media to design a way to live doing pretty much whatever we like. By eliminating space, Internet technology allows the whole world to participate in shaping the future.

So if we are living in a “global village”, why are we unable to connect with others as substantially and meaningfully as we once did? This is because technological evolution leads to the isolation of the individual and the fragmentation of society. This process started with the invention of the printing press and the creation of the literate “public. This redefined communication, making it a private affair rather than human interaction. With the advent of electronic media, the public evolved into the masses, as identical information was able to be conveyed to large amounts of people whether they were literate or not. With computers and the Internet, users are plunged into a world of over stimulation, and rather than being able to comprehend all of the information they must rely on pattern recognition and filtering to cope with the high volume of input. This leads people to put little to no value on information, since it is so abundant in every part of their lives. Rather than using this new medium as a way to solve problems and engage the world, most choose to use it as an escape from their overstimulated lives in the form of mass entertainment. The amount of violence and corruption users are exposed to eventually makes them numb, and incapable of expressing true emotions. Emotions have become something we mimic from television rather than real displays of human feelings. Were we to mimic actors such as Keanu Reeves in any of his popular films, we would reflect wooden, emotionless beings. Attempting to contribute to the mass of information available on the Internet seems to now be a futile gesture. Either your information is already available, or it's doomed to be lost in the information overload. By connecting the whole world, we have only succeeded in creating a giant feedback loop that signifies nothing. If this is how our new technology operates, why are we so obsessed with it?

In “Understanding Media,” McLuhan discusses our obsession with technology by relating it to the Greek myth of Narcissus. In the myth, Narcissus falls in love with a reflection of himself in the water, and becomes numb to everything else because of it. It is commonly misunderstood to mean he fell in love with himself. What it really shows is that he fell in love with his reflection without knowing it was a reflection of him. Like Narcissus, we become obsessed with media because media are extensions of the human body. The book and television are extensions of the eye, the radio of the ear, and electric circuitry an extension of the central nervous system. We may not normally identify them as such, but it is easy to recognize the extensions. Like Narcissus, we are numbed by these extensions of ourselves, and this numbness contributes to an environment of fixation on media. As long as we remain obsessed with technology, we won't be able to use it to its full extent. We will continue to be shortsighted in our employment of technology, and keep living in a state of numb fixation.

So how does McLuhan and his ideas affect us and tie into our daily lives? Media are an inescapable part of our lives, and if we better understand media we can use it to our advantage. We must use it as a tool to decide how best to use developing technologies. If we do not engage media, it will enslave its passive users. We are on the brink of becoming obsolete if we do not choose to use media and technology wisely. Marshall McLuhan had a lot of good ideas about media, but we must move further to fully realize the power of media, and how it infiltrates every aspect of our lives. Without this understanding, we will remain on a course racing towards disaster.
